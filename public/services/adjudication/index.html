Construction Adjudication

A fast, statutory process for resolving disputes under construction contracts — most often about payment. Steve acts as adjudicator, or advises and represents parties.

What is construction adjudication?

Adjudication is a statutory dispute resolution procedure for construction contracts in the UK. It was introduced by Part II of the Housing Grants, Construction and Regeneration Act 1996 (the "Construction Act"), which came into force on 1 May 1998, and was amended in England and Wales by Part 8 of the Local Democracy, Economic Development and Construction Act 2009 for contracts entered into on or after 1 October 2011.

A party to a construction contract within the Act has the right to refer a dispute to adjudication at any time. If the contract does not contain compliant adjudication provisions, the relevant parts of the Scheme for Construction Contracts are implied into it.

How the process works

  1. Notice of Adjudication. The referring party gives written notice of its intention to refer a dispute, describing the dispute and the redress sought.
  2. Appointment. An adjudicator is named in the contract, agreed between the parties, or nominated by an adjudicator nominating body.
  3. Referral. The referral notice, with supporting documents, must be served within seven days of the notice of adjudication.
  4. Response and submissions. The responding party answers, and the adjudicator may request further submissions, documents or a meeting.
  5. Decision. The adjudicator must reach a decision within 28 days of the referral. This can be extended by up to 14 days with the referring party's consent, or longer if both parties agree.

The decision is binding until the dispute is finally determined by legal proceedings, arbitration or agreement. If it is not paid, the successful party can usually enforce it through the Technology and Construction Court by way of summary judgment.

Payment disputes, smash and grab and true value

Most adjudications concern money. The Construction Act requires payment notices and, where the payer intends to pay less than the sum due, a valid pay less notice served in time. Where a payer fails to serve the required notices, the "notified sum" may become payable regardless of the true value of the work — the basis of so-called smash and grab adjudications.

A payer who has missed its notices may later bring a true value adjudication to establish what was actually due. Following the Court of Appeal's decision in Grove Developments Ltd v S&T (UK) Ltd [2018] EWCA Civ 2448, the payer must generally pay the notified sum before it can pursue a true value adjudication. Timing, notices and contract terms all matter, and each case turns on its facts.

Disputes commonly referred

  • Interim and final account valuations
  • Payment notices and pay less notices
  • Variations and additional work
  • Extensions of time and liquidated damages
  • Loss and expense
  • Defects and the cost of remedial work
  • Termination and repudiatory breach

Who adjudication does not cover

Contracts with a residential occupier for work on the dwelling they live in, or intend to live in, are excluded from the statutory scheme. Homeowners and their builders can still agree to adjudication by including an adjudication clause in their contract.

Frequently asked questions

How long does construction adjudication take?

The adjudicator must reach a decision within 28 days of the referral notice. That can be extended by up to 14 days if the referring party agrees, or for longer if both parties agree.

Is an adjudicator's decision binding?

Yes, on an interim basis. The decision must be complied with unless and until the dispute is finally determined by litigation, arbitration or agreement. Courts generally enforce adjudication decisions promptly, with limited exceptions such as a lack of jurisdiction or a material breach of natural justice.

Can a homeowner use adjudication?

Not as a statutory right where the contract is with a residential occupier for work on their own home. The parties can, however, agree to adjudication in their contract.

What is the difference between a smash and grab and a true value adjudication?

A smash and grab adjudication claims the notified sum because the payer failed to serve a valid payment or pay less notice in time. A true value adjudication asks the adjudicator to decide what was actually due. Following Grove v S&T [2018], the payer must generally pay the notified sum before starting a true value adjudication.

Who pays the costs of adjudication?

Each party normally bears its own costs. The adjudicator decides how their own fees and expenses are to be shared between the parties.
